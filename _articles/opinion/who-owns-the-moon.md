The law on the books is simple: no one owns the Moon. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty says it is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by use, by occupation, or by any other means. What that rule does not answer is what happens when governments and private companies start building real infrastructure there.

I think a company like SpaceX will get there first. Capitalism breeds innovation by allocating resources to the most valuable products or resources. The law was written for flags and governments, not for landers, mines, and bases.

## The 1967 Outer Space Treaty

Article II of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty is the line everyone quotes, but the rest of the treaty matters too. Article VI makes countries responsible for national activities on the Moon, including private companies, which need authorization and continuing supervision. Article IX requires due regard for others, care to avoid harmful contamination, and consultations over potentially harmful interference. Article XII says stations and equipment on the Moon must be open to other treaty parties on a reciprocal basis, with reasonable advance notice. You cannot plant a flag and call the ground yours, and you cannot pretend a company is acting on its own.

## The treaty almost no one joined

The 1979 Moon Agreement calls the Moon and its natural resources the "common heritage of mankind." It says the surface, the subsurface, and resources still in place cannot become property, and that a base does not create ownership of the land. It called for an international regime, including equitable benefit-sharing, when mining became feasible. As of January 1, 2026, it has only 17 parties. The United States, China, and Russia never joined.

## You can't own the land. You may own what you take.

The 2015 U.S. Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act says a U.S. citizen who commercially recovers a space resource may possess, own, transport, use, and sell it, consistent with U.S. and international law. Congress also said the act does not assert sovereignty or ownership of any celestial body. Luxembourg's 2017 law says space resources are capable of being owned. Japan's 2021 law lets a licensed operator own resources mined under an approved plan.

The Artemis Accords, adopted October 13, 2020, are political commitments, not a United Nations treaty. On October 28, 2026, Vietnam became the 78th nation to sign. The Accords say extraction does not inherently amount to national appropriation under Article II. They also provide for "safety zones," areas where normal operations or an accident could reasonably interfere with someone else's work. The zones are supposed to be temporary, and signatories still commit to free access across celestial bodies.

That is the tension. You cannot own the land. You may own what you extract. And a safety zone around a base can start to look like control.

I do not think that is an accident. Land claiming will never cease. People have locks on their doors because they don't like sharing. Safety zones and resource rights will, in practice, turn into de facto claims. The law should be honest about that.

## Two tracks, one clock

China and Russia are not among those 78 signatories. On March 9, 2021, they signed a memorandum to build an International Lunar Research Station. In April 2025, China's space administration said a basic model is planned by 2035 at the lunar south pole, with an extended model in the 2040s. Chang'e-7 and Chang'e-8 will be part of it.

![Illustration of two rival lunar bases, one blue and one red, facing each other across a crystal-filled crater](/assets/images/who-owns-the-moon-south-pole-race.jpg "Illustration: AI-assisted image of two rival lunar bases facing each other across a crater.")

Hardware is already moving. India's Chandrayaan-3 made the first soft landing near the south pole in 2023, and China's Chang'e-6 returned the first far-side samples in 2024. In 2025, Firefly Aerospace's Blue Ghost 1 landed successfully, and Intuitive Machines' IM-2 tipped onto its side near the south pole. Artemis II flew four astronauts around the far side in April 2026, a flyby, not a landing. In August 2026, China postponed Chang'e-7, saying it could not fly in this year's planned window.

## Set the rules first

I do not expect people to stop wanting a lock on the door. I do expect governments to stop pretending that "no one owns the Moon" will be enough once companies and countries start building there. The rules should be set before the infrastructure arrives, not after. If we wait until the first real base is already up, we will not be writing law. We will be arguing over a claim that is already on the ground.
