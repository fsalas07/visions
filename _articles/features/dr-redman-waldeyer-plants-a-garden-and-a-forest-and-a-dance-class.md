As Dr. Christine Redman-Waldeyer settles into her new role as Director of Paterson’s Poetry Center, at Passaic County Community College’s Hamilton Club, she reflects on what she envisions for the Center, and how she has arrived here. At the time of the interview, she was 10 weeks into the position that began at the end of February 2026.

She has already judged a few book contests and made Poetry Center history by electing the first child winner of one such contest.

The winning author submitted a children’s book of poetry, written for her peers. It included original poetry as a guide for other children to begin their own poems. Dr. Redman-Waldeyer lauded it as “really beautiful,” calling it “one of the highlights” of her Poetry Center so far.

Perhaps there is some of Dr. Redman-Waldeyer mirrored in the young author’s book, as she too hopes to provide an example and space for people to create their own poetry.

If the Poetry Center is a tree, former Executive Director Maria Mazziotti Gillan first laid the roots. Now, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer builds from there as well as from the roots of her father and her childhood, and branches out to spaces all her own.

Dr. Redman-Waldeyer draws from childhood questions of identity, ballet classes and gardens. She moves through exploring wild places by the beach where she lives and understanding her own family’s history. She brings all of this to the space she’s creating at the Poetry Center.

As a girl, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer remembers attending church.

**CRW (Christine Redman-Waldeyer):** I wanted to be an altar girl. And it never occurred to me…it was all boys. I was just like, “Oh! I am going to do that.” And then I was turned down by our priest.

This greatly upset her and began a quest for understanding what spaces and roles were for her. Her quest could have gone very differently if it wasn’t for her father.

Rather than accept the gendered limitation, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer’s dad made so much room for young Christine’s ideas. After she was denied being an altar girl, her family “went church hopping” until they found one where she could serve on the altar.

This was one pivotal moment, set in action by her idea and encouraged by her dad, that helped put Dr. Redman-Waldeyer on the path that leads to where she is today.

**AB (Arianne Bakelmun):** Why does the connection to your dad feel important for your role at the Poetry Center?

**CRW:** He never said, “No” to me. \[Aside\] from dangerous things.

“I want to plant this in my garden.”

“Sure!”

“Can I have this pet?”

“Sure!”

I had every animal under the sun.

Dr. Redman-Waldeyer aims to bring this freedom of exploration to the Poetry Center.

**CRW:** My father let me, in the summers, grow my garden. He had a little plot of land he gave me, and I planted whatever I wanted…It’s almost like writers need to have that garden where they can plant anything and figure out if it works or not.

Even if it becomes the “beautiful disaster” that culminated the year Christine planted pumpkins.

**CRW:** It climbed all over the place in our neighbors’ yards, and I’ll never forget \[they\] weren’t mad. My father couldn’t mow the lawn because this thing had gone all over. It went into the neighbor’s yard, and they made pumpkin pies out of it. They were having a good time with it. They were not upset and, would I plant the pumpkins again? Probably not. But it’s like that idea, if students of writing can just be like, “Let me try this out and maybe it works or maybe it doesn’t.” And maybe there’s a beautiful outcome even in the disaster and chaos of that writing exercise.

Dr. Redman-Waldeyer believes this varied exploration helped cultivate her voice. Now, she is concerned that the “elephant in the room, AI, it’s stealing our precious voices.”

**CRW:** We’re not confident. I am talking in general. I think we’re using it over and over again…

It’s useful in many ways, but I think over-reliance on it is dangerous. If you are a journalist right now, there is a vibe and a machine can’t have that human reaction to that vibe. You pick something up as a human being in the room.

Dr. Redman-Waldeyer wrote a textbook, “Unlocking Your Authentic Voice,” published in 2026, which came from this conflict. She hopes to impart to her students and to everyone who participates at the Poetry Center, “Let’s keep our voices.”

She stresses that “your story matters.” Dr. Redman-Waldeyer reflects that Maria Gillan, former Executive Director of the Poetry Center, has cultivated the Center as a space where such stories are shared. Gillan led by example, writing and telling of her experience as an Italian immigrant growing up in Paterson. The genuine quality of Gillan’s voice resonates with readers in a way that Dr. Redman-Waldeyer values.

**CRW:** She told her story and it resonates with the audience…We should never underestimate the power of words. Stories have the ability to connect us in ways nothing else can.

In her Creative Dissertation from Drew University, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer emphasized the importance of the reader/writer relationship. The authenticity of the writer makes a reader see themselves in the story and feel less alone.

**CRW:** Fiction or nonfiction…even if it’s made up, I think all characters tell a truth…it is always holding a truth for us.

At the Poetry Center, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer imagines a space that would hold that truth for anyone who visits.

**CRW:** It’s almost like we’re so busy in our lives, the busyness of the city and our computers. And then we want to go camping, or we want to go into nature, and we want to go on a hike. We want to be one-on-one with the world.

And I feel like the Poetry Center is going to become that. That’s going to be a getaway. That’s going to be us walking in nature, touching the leaves, smelling the flowers, watching the birds, being with the earth. And I think writing is earthy. And it’s even if it’s held in a building and even if there are artificial lights, it’s like bringing us out into the woods, so we can be in the forest of ourselves.

Much like the nourishment we find in nature, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer knows we need nourishment through art.

**CRW:** \[The Poetry Center\] is still going to be that space where…we talk to each other through poetry. Art is so important; it captures things we can’t say in regular language and prose. Grief. To say I’m sad isn’t enough. Loving something or someone isn’t enough. Metaphor, putting it in poetic form, putting it in art allows us to pinpoint exactly that feeling.

**AB:** Why is pinpointing exactly that feeling beyond “I’m sad,” important?

**CRW:** I think I have an answer. I used to love ballet and I did ballet as a child. So, I started up again, right? And it’s like you’re going into a stretch of one of those muscles that you didn’t know was there, but then you are like, ‘Oh! That’s an important part of my body.’

It’s like if you could put it in that language. The language finds that little muscle of emotion.

It’s not a material thing you can touch or feel, but it’s definitely a sort of muscle that you are like, “Ohh, I didn’t know that was there. That feels good to stretch that out.”

By stretching out an emotion, we bring it into our awareness and feel more whole. We can now use that muscle to dance, like we use poetry to express, and allow a beautiful connection. Dancer and audience, writer and reader, can resonate with a more whole expression of themselves.

From being a rebellious altar girl, planting wildly in her childhood garden, to nature explorations and dance classes, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer remains driven by big ideas and big expressions.

**CRW:** I drive my family crazy. I am like, “Let me try this, let me try that.” I am still planting crazy stuff in my garden.

Around seven years ago, she experimentally planted a type of Italian squash.

**CRW:** I am not joking when I say this thing grew to be five feet long. It overtook my entire garden and squished everything else out. We had a good time picking those. We didn’t know they were terrible tasting.

…Well, that’s AI to me. It’s squeezing out all the other vegetables. And that’s bringing us back to my textbook I wrote for composition classes; don’t let that happen. Be careful what you plant in your gardens.

It’s a balancing act, to allow wildness and experimentation while being mindful not to crowd out important growth.

**AB:** In the language of plants, what roots do you hope to strengthen that are already within the Poetry Center? And what new growth do you hope you’ll be able to bring?

**CRW:** So in the first few weeks of June, I am going to be running a \[memoir\] workshop. Normally we do focus on narrative poetry, so I kind of see memoir as an extension of narrative poetry. I have poetry and memoir pieces and I like to blend it together.

To go back to ballet, poetry is like packing things tight, really tightening those muscles. Sometimes you need to stretch those arms and jump. I feel like the memoir will let us jump a little bit; we’ll do a bigger stretch.

In this way, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer brings some of herself to the Poetry Center. She also envisions more student engagement.

**CRW:** Dr. Rose \[former President of Passaic County Community College\], who unfortunately passed this past March, was a really big blow to me. He pulled me in and talked to me about his vision for the Poetry Center. I know it’s hard with community college students because they’re caught up with working and just trying to get by to get their degree. But if they could take a Saturday morning, or whatever, and say maybe this is for me too.

I know Dr. Rose really wanted to see our students benefit from our center and the arts. And so, I hope I can do him justice.

He loved the center. He was very proud of the center \[and\] the college newspaper, and that’s one of the reasons he encouraged me to take this over. I believe people are watching even when they pass. I believe they’re with us still, so I hope I make them proud.

If Dr. Redman-Waldeyer has made anyone proud, her dad is among them.

**AB:** What would your father think about you being the director of the Poetry Center?

**CRW:** He is smiling from ear to ear. You can actually get a job in poetry!

Her father’s enthusiastic “Sure!” encouraged Dr. Redman-Waldeyer beyond gardens and pets.

**CRW:** Whatever I wanted to plan. When I wanted to be an English major, he’s like, “Sure!” And then when I finished my bachelor’s program, he’s like, “So now what’s next? Are you going to get your master’s? You should do your master’s.”

And I was like, “Yeah. I should do my master’s!” Then I got the master’s. And he wasn’t pushy.

But he’s like, “What’s next?” And he’s like, “What about your doctorate?”

And I am like, “Yeah. Yeah. I should do that too.”

He was always like, “What’s your next step? What are you going to do?” He was always encouraging me to explore.

I was in my master’s program when I did a genealogy study. It wound up being my thesis. The chair of my committee was not very happy that I changed my topic. I just felt I needed to do this. They resolved to let me do this study, even though I was like partially through the other. But I think my dad was just so happy.

He liked my research and he liked me exploring. I just felt like I was always on an expedition.

The expedition into identity is something that started young. When she was around 10 years old, an Indian family moved next door to her in their very white neighborhood. They became great friends, and witnessing their foods and culture sparked Christine’s curiosity about her own.

**CRW:** Who am I in this? What are my roots? I grew up with Shake ‘n Bake chicken and canned beans. There were no traditional dishes or customs.

This too was something her father hungered for, also wondering, “Who am I? Why is my hair black?”

**CRW:** I think he didn’t really feel comfortable in his skin either.

This unlocked a lifelong dive into her family history. A DNA test uncovered English, Dutch and Native American ancestry on her mother’s side. Dr. Redman-Waldeyer learned of her maternal distant uncle, Abraham Clark, one of the signers of the Declaration of Independence.

When her paternal grandmother died, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer recalls going through her attic with her dad. They discovered together that his mom was Czechoslovakian and his father was Spanish.

Her family identity feels “like an octopus; there is the brain of it, but there are so many more arms to explore.”

**CRW:** That’s a very American thing. We are a country of all different backgrounds, and that’s beautiful to have contact with so many different cultures.

At the same time, she reflects, “You might never feel totally at home in your one culture.”

After Dr. Redman-Waldeyer’s research found her father’s relatives in Gibraltar, she was able to gift him the experience of traveling to meet his uncle and cousins that he never knew existed.

**CRW:** I am glad he left the earth getting to do that.

The Poetry Center will continue to be a home for people who are searching for identity.

Dr. Redman-Waldeyer wants it to be a space to dream. Her own dreaming almost led her to open a bed-and-breakfast, envisioning hosting writing retreats under her former woman-focused literary journal, *Adanna*.

That plan fell through. She didn’t realize at the time, but it was another parallel with her father, who also was thwarted in his dream of opening a bed-and-breakfast.

Now, however, as the new Director, Dr. Redman-Waldeyer will host writing at the Poetry Center.

**CRW:** I feel like I got my dream come true. It just came to me in a different way.
